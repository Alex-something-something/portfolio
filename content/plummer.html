Measuring Coupling in a Bistable Physical Ising Model

This one-dimensional physical Ising system uses bistable buckling beams as two-state mechanical elements. The effective coupling coefficient, Jeffective, describes how the energy and preferred state of a beam are influenced by its nearest neighbors. I developed a new “perfect pin” model from the experimental restrictions and possible sources of inaccuracy identified in the previous thesis.

I designed the specimen and configuration test matrix, developed the test procedure, operated the Instron 68SC-5, processed the force data, and produced the resulting plots. The campaign comprised 48 tests across four models: one legacy model and three concepts I developed. It included eight initial configurations per model, synchronized camera trials of high and low energy configurations, three additional trials of each of four high energy configurations, and two reversed uneven-neighbor configurations.

Measured forces spanned 0–181 mN, with peak buckling forces of 9–181 mN. Comparisons between configurations with identical immediate neighbors but different next-nearest-neighbor states confirmed that the next-nearest-neighbor contribution to buckling energy is minimal within the tested system. The percentage difference remains withheld while data processing continues.

Before this campaign, the laboratory's empirical input for the model came from measurements of buckled-beam curvature; coupling had not been directly investigated through force testing. Because the force required to trigger buckling is directly proportional to the relevant derivative of internal energy, I synchronized camera recordings with the force response to correlate beam curvature and force. The resulting dataset provides the laboratory's first direct force measurements intended to support a future empirical calculation of Jeffective and a more representative analytical or simulation model. Coupling coefficients have not yet been calculated.

The joint trade study compared ball-bearing, tight-tolerance LEGO-based, and fixed resin-printed concepts. Instron results showed greater scatter, inconsistent behavior, and cases in which an adjacent beam buckled instead of the directly loaded beam in the rotating concepts. My fixed pins, printed on an Elegoo Mars 5 Ultra and installed through a screw-retained slide-in interface, produced more controlled buckling while restricting unintended translation, rotation, and off-axis twisting.

Comparison with the mathematical model is ongoing. The resulting force plot is being evaluated for inclusion in future laboratory publication; exact configuration data, force comparisons, and model parameters remain unpublished.

Structural Design & Dynamic Testing

I design and manufacture mechanical fixtures for dynamic research experiments, supporting design reviews with analysis, cost comparison, and tolerance-driven fabrication planning. I reduced estimated hardware cost from $1,170 outsourced to $71.11 through in-house fabrication and fastener selection. The in-house cost comprised $3.96 for bolts and $67.15 for the plate.

  • Material Trade Study: Pivoted a shaker-adapter design from an orthotropic honeycomb matrix to a solid 1/4 in 6061-T6 aluminum plate, improving confidence in isotropic failure-mode characterization and reducing manufacturing risk.
  • Dynamic Fixture Design: Designed an adapter for a physical Ising-model test fixture, predicting a 0.44 mm maximum deflection under a 3 kg load at 20.1 Hz using SolidWorks Dynamic FEA.
  • Fastener Analysis: Calculated preload and thread-shear performance for Class 8.8 and stainless hardware, calculating a 21.7 ft·lbf tightening torque to achieve the intended Class 8.8 bolt preload and confirming a 1.49 FOS for 3,710 lbf peak forces at 11 G and a minimum 2.46 FOS for thread, plate-bearing, and pull-through modes.

Precision Manufacturing

Generated G-code and machined the 6061-T6 adapter on a Haas VF-2 CNC mill. The finished geometry included a circular, chopped-end conical chamfer across the underside, corner fillets, and 12 drilled holes held to ± 0.005 in tolerances. Removing material from the 3.19 kg stock produced a 1.90 kg finished part, a 40.4% stock-to-part mass reduction.

Applied GD&T and worst-case tolerance-stack analysis, calculated ISO 273 clearances for ISO 4017 M10 hardware, and added 0.5 mm edge breaks.

I presented a mini design review of the shaker adapter, covering design iterations, finished hardware, and results, as my flash-talk update at a multi-lab research meeting.

Laboratory Commissioning & Apparatus

I supported the operational build-out of a mechanics-of-materials research laboratory by evaluating and procuring equipment, tooling, and supplies against a $3,500 startup budget.

  • High-Speed Camera Mount: Designed, built, and used an adjustable overhead stand from 45 × 45 mm 80/20 aluminum extrusion. Calculated deflection below 0.1 mm under a conservative load of twice the expected 2.0 kg static camera mass. Rigidity and axial-buckling calculations used a 68 GPa Young’s modulus and 106,217 mm⁴ area moment of inertia; this was calculated performance, not a measured deflection result.
  • Cost-Conscious Design: Prioritized long, modular extrusion stock that could be reused in more stable future apparatus. Balanced a $390 budget, six-foot stock lengths, and freight constraints while preserving 100% material reusability. The stand was an investment in the laboratory’s future equipment-building capability.
