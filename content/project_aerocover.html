My Role: Define the Trade, Then Narrow It

I lead development of Argo’s external downcomer fairing, setting the shared requirements and analysis plan. I developed preliminary Icarus baseline calculations, screened aerodynamic and thermal loads, investigated mounting and manufacturing options, and led the September 3, 2026 conceptual design review.

I coordinated three concept studies against shared vehicle constraints. Quinn Colleran, Hannah Ruan, and Aaron Lin own the individual concept geometries credited below; I developed the common analysis, integration, and manufacturing trade.

  • Packaging: 63 in fairing length and a nominal 1.6 in height around the downcomer on an 8 in outer-diameter rocket, with space for external wiring. Final clearance awaits the fluids team’s released geometry.
  • Flight environment: CoDR design point of approximately 28.75 psi maximum dynamic pressure; compare candidates through Mach 2.0–2.81.
  • Selection criteria: Integrated drag, thermal and structural survival, attachment integrity, mold cost, dimensional control, and surface finish.

Design Philosophy: Leading Edge, Midsection, Boattail

I organized the team’s first-principles study around three regions, allowing us to compare leading profiles, midsection cross-sections, and aft tapers independently before combining promising features. We also considered complete body profiles, whose geometry is defined from front to back rather than assembled from separate regions.

What the diagram means: it identifies the drag mechanisms each region initially brings into focus. The “dominates” labels are a teaching simplification, not a quantified allocation of the fairing’s drag.

  • Leading edge: Focus on wave/pressure drag, with length, blunting, heating, and fabrication considered together.
  • Midsection: Focus on wetted area and skin friction while maintaining downcomer/wiring clearance and a smooth rocket-body junction.
  • Boattail: Balance the smaller base/wake against pressure forces on the taper and separation risk. Expansion changes the pressure distribution; it is not an additional independent drag term to add twice.

Full revolution ≠ mounted fairing: an optimum isolated axisymmetric body does not necessarily remain optimal when only part of that shape is joined to the rocket. Body-junction flow, the upstream boundary layer, attachment details, and nearby fins couple the regions. Published profiles establish candidates; integrated CFD must establish their ranking.

Why Optimize Around Mach 2–2.81?

I used the trajectory to prioritize the flight conditions where a drag improvement is most consequential. Drag follows D = q Sref CD, with q = ½ρV²: time at a Mach number alone does not describe its contribution to aerodynamic loading.

The trajectory motivated comparison at Mach 2.0, 2.5, and 2.81 near the high-dynamic-pressure supersonic interval. Other regimes remain in the trade; their drag contribution depends on both dynamic pressure and the changing drag coefficient.

Mounting & Manufacturing Trade

I compared carbon-fiber wet layup on printed positive molds with machined insulation-foam tooling, considering cost, internal fit, release, cure conditions, and repeatable surface finish. I also evaluated mold polarity and vacuum consolidation using lessons from the previous cover.

For attachments, I investigated recessed fasteners with a flush surface treatment and underside bonded hardware. The trade includes local laminate thickness, access, load paths, and support along the cover; removing a protruding screw is only useful if the attachment remains practical and structurally sound.

The CoDR established manufacturing options and integration questions. It did not close out the final mold, laminate, or attachment design.

Thermal Screening & Validation Plan

I calculated preliminary stagnation and recovery temperatures to flag the mismatch between the aerodynamic environment and the available epoxy’s temperature capability. These are gas-temperature screening estimates; they do not establish the laminate’s transient temperature or prove thermal failure.

Thermal hand calculations & assumptions

T₀ = T∞ [1 + (γ − 1)M∞² / 2]

Tlocal = T₀ / [1 + (γ − 1)Mlocal² / 2]

Tr = Tlocal [1 + r(γ − 1)Mlocal² / 2]

At Mach 2.81, static temperature 236 K, and γ = 1.4, the ideal-gas stagnation relation gives approximately 609 K (636°F). A separate recovery estimate used local Mach 2 and a recovery factor of 0.89, giving approximately 579 K (583°F).

Exposure duration, heat transfer, laminate construction, and the specific cured resin still need to be assessed. The CoDR’s roughly 120–125°F epoxy limit is a preliminary material assumption to confirm for the selected resin and cure.

Reference: NASA Glenn isentropic flow relations.

I am designing a curved carbon-fiber coupon with a bolted interface, held under sustained load while heated, to observe local dimpling and loss of attachment stiffness. This will inform a conservative operating limit for that configuration; testing and final material selection remain pending.

Related Investigation: Argo Nosecone Shape

The nosecone belongs to the separation and recovery subsystem; this section documents the aerodynamic comparison. Any selected shape must also preserve the recovery packaging and deployment interfaces.

The fairing trade led me to investigate the vehicle’s nosecone using RASAero II. I swept nine profile families and tip-radius inputs for Argo’s 40 in long, 8 in diameter nosecone (L/D = 5), then repeated the comparison at L/D = 3 to investigate a ranking difference from the published foredrag curves used in our CoDR.

RASAero II screening predicted approximately 1.1% higher apogee for the ¾-power and Von Kármán profiles than the tangent-ogive baseline. These whole-vehicle outputs do not isolate nose drag; a common-input, drag-coefficient, and stability comparison remains pending.

No replacement nosecone has been selected. The sweep and tip-radius sensitivity are retained below as preliminary simulation evidence.
